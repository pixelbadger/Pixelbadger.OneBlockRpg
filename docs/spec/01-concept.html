1 · Concept & research

The one city block RPG: origin, definition, attempts, and why an LLM-backed text engine is a plausible form of it.

1.1 The idea

Warren Spector is the designer and producer behind Ultima Underworld, System Shock, Thief and, as director, Deus Ex. For more than a decade he has described one "dream game": an RPG that takes place within a single city block, simulated in exhaustive detail. In his own words:

"My ultimate dream is for someone to be foolish enough to give me the money to make what I call the One Block Role-Playing Game, where we simulate one building, one city block perfectly."

"I really want deep worlds that you can interact with. My ideal game would be one city block – some day I'm going to make that." (to The Guardian, 2012)

Both quotes appear in Jim Rossignol's 2012 Rock Paper Shotgun feature (§1.3). A later description (Edge #395, 2024) spells out what would be simulated:

  • Take one US-style city block and simulate every building on it.
  • Simulate the shops and apartments inside those buildings and every object and appliance inside them.
  • Simulate the infrastructure that connects them, such as power and water supplies.
  • Simulate the people who live and work there, with their personalities, routines, relationships and even their pets.

Spector's own framing contrasts the two directions a game can scale in. Most open-world games go "miles wide and an inch deep". He wants to go "an inch wide and a mile deep, so you can really do anything you can think of". He puts the emphasis on non-combat AI and on NPCs "who all have their own stories". Physics and systemic interaction matter too, but they are secondary to the people.

1.3 Designers on the idea (RPS, 2012)

Jim Rossignol's Will We Ever Get To Play "One City Block"? (Rock Paper Shotgun, 31 Oct 2012) asked developers from Arkane and Fullbright why the idea keeps its pull. Their answers point directly at design requirements:

WhoPointImplication for us
Harvey Smith (Deus Ex, Dishonored)"It's a clever constraint, like the Twilight Zone episode where everything takes place in one room." The key question is whether it would feel like "an emergent narrative in the sense of a game like FTL or … XCOM, or more like an 'insanely scripted' RPG … there's a thrill and some feeling of elegance to game interactions when they arise from systems." This is the central design choice. We choose systems first, with the LLM director shaping rather than scripting (P8). The Smith quote is the best one-line statement of why.
Christophe Carrier (Dishonored lead level designer)"Tight and dense with consequences … the tighter the space, the more detail is possible … a world where little things matter and that you leave traces of your influence everywhere." He also wants to "use the bathrooms or turn the AC on, even if there's no gameplay implications whatsoever." Mundane interactions are first-class even with no mechanical payoff (P3). Player actions leave persistent, discoverable traces (P6).
Steve Gaynor (Gone Home)"Too many games are much too broad and shallow." But he adds that the full idea "implies a lot more: simulating the people who live and work there, allowing the player to buy equipment and improve their character's skills and attributes." Being a CRPG means character progression and an economy, not just exploration. The draft spec is thin here (Q16).
Raphael Colantonio (Arkane)"Ultima Underworld set in a modern mall, blocked off and sealed up after a cataclysm … with rival clans and factional alliances." A sealed setting justifies the hard boundary (P1). Factions give the director tensions to work with.
Jim Rossignol"Would there be a single bullet in a revolver in a drawer somewhere, which would signal the end of the game when it was fired?" It is "not simply a technical challenge … it's a design challenge." Scarce, significant objects work as dramatic constraints. The hard part is making depth fun, not just making it exist.

1.4 Why it has never shipped

Three costs scale badly with depth:

  1. Simulation cost. Every object must behave plausibly under any use. In 3D this means physics, animation, rendering and interaction affordances for thousands of things.
  2. Authoring cost. Every resident needs a history, voice and reactions to anything the player might say or do. Branching dialogue grows combinatorially.
  3. Coherence cost. Emergent stories from deep systems are usually noise rather than narrative. Something has to shape what happens into plot without scripting it.

1.5 Why a text engine with LLMs changes the equation

CostHow this project attacks it
SimulationText removes the rendering and physics tax. An object needs a state and some rules, not a mesh and a collider. A kettle can be {filled, powered, temperature} in a few lines. This makes "every object and appliance" tractable.
AuthoringLLMs voice the residents. Authors (human or LLM-assisted, at world-generation time) define who a character is: identity, history, secrets, goals and relationships. The model produces what they say in response to anything, grounded in what they know.
CoherenceAn LLM "director" reads the simulation and nudges it. It proposes plot beats that exploit existing tensions, such as a debt, an affair, a leaking pipe or a power outage. It never invents facts outside the rules.

The central risk runs the other way. LLMs are too willing to invent. A model that freely makes up objects, facts and outcomes gives you improv theatre, not a simulation, and the "mile deep" promise collapses because nothing is consistent. So the design constraint that runs through this spec is the simulation is authoritative; the model is a voice and an advisor (see §2). This is Harvey Smith's systems-over-script choice (§1.3) in modern form.

1.7 What we take from each precedent

  • Gone Home: every drawer and cabinet carries meaning, and history is discovered through objects.
  • Immersive sims (Looking Glass, Ion Storm, Arkane): systemic objects, multiple solutions and player expression over designer intent.
  • Disco Elysium: a small place made dense through conversation and history. Every noun is a door into story.
  • Generative Agents: memory stream → retrieval → reflection → planning as a model for resident minds, and cheap offscreen behaviour versus expensive on-stage behaviour.
  • Classic parser IF (Infocom, Inform): a world model of rooms, objects and containment, and a turn loop. The parser is the part the LLM improves.
