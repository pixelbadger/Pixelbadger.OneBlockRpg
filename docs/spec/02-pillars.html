2 · Design pillars

The principles every feature is tested against. If a feature violates a pillar, it needs an explicit exception.

The premise

Consciousness is a non-real percept. What an LLM does and what people do to construct a coherent social reality are similar acts of construction.

The game takes this literally. A small, simple simulation grounds the world: what exists, where it is, and what state it's in. On top of that, characters and the player construct social reality in conversation: what things mean, who owes whom, what people believe, what is rumoured, what is uncanny. That layer has no limits of tone. The hyperreal and the magically real are welcome. It touches the ground only where the author has declared that it may.

P1 · Bounded space, unbounded depth

The playable world MUST be one city block. It grows inward, through more detail, history and meaning, never outward. Leaving is impossible: the edge of the block is impassable, and no payload can declare an exit off it (Q6).

P2 · Ground and construct

  • Ground: rooms, objects, properties, locations, flags and time. These are owned by the engine and are deterministic. LLM output MUST NOT change them directly.
  • Construct: dialogue, claims, beliefs and meaning. Generated by LLM-driven characters, and unbounded in content.
  • Bridge: the constructed becomes grounded only through hooks the author declares in the payload, such as set_flag, give, unlock_exit or add_belief. The LLM decides when to invoke a hook. The engine checks its guards and applies it. If a character insists there is a 13th floor, it only exists if the author gave them a hook that can make it so.

P3 · A simple simulation

  • Rooms are descriptive. They have no dimensions or coordinates, just a description, contents and exits.
  • Objects are in rooms (or in other objects, or held by characters). They carry simulable properties such as mass, velocity, temperature and open/locked. Rules read and write those properties.
  • Fidelity comes from authored detail and well-chosen properties, not from physics. If a mechanic needs geometry, it is the wrong mechanic for this engine.

P4 · Everything acts the same way

There is one action vocabulary (go, take, drop, put, open, use, throw, give, say, wait…). The player, scripted objects and characters all act through it, under the same rules. A clock can chime, a dog can wander and a kettle can boil using scripted behaviours. Characters are objects that also have goals, intents, relationships, beliefs and SPECIAL. Their everyday behaviour, such as routines and reactions, is scripted like any other object's.

P5 · The LLM speaks in conversation, and directs set pieces

LLM work happens in conversations, in declared set pieces where a director orchestrates the cast (§6.7), in explicitly declared scripted callouts (§6.5), and in nightly day summaries (§6.8). Everything else, including room and object descriptions, action results and behaviour, is authored text in the payload. That text is written ahead of time, usually by a coding agent. This keeps play cheap, fast, deterministic and testable without a model.

P6 · Conversations are structured

Conversations are multiple choice. The player picks from options that mix authored lines from the payload (pinned at plot points, gated by conditions and SPECIAL) with LLM-generated lines. The character is driven by its high-level goals plus conversation-specific goals from the payload. Characters can act mid-conversation, for example walking away, handing something over or throwing a punch, using the shared action vocabulary (§6.6). Conversations can trigger scripted behaviours, combat and further LLM work.

P7 · Authored story, systemic play

The story comes from the author: plot, characters, relationships and tensions, and trigger points. Triggers are declarative condition → effect rules. Play emerges where behaviours, conversations and player actions collide with those triggers. This is Harvey Smith's choice between the "insanely scripted" and the systemic (§1.3), answered with a hybrid: an authored skeleton with systemic flesh. For set pieces, the author gives the player a loose objective and the director a brief, and the director orchestrates the event within declared limits.

P8 · Agent-authorable

A game is a payload: data validated against a published schema (§7). It MUST be possible for a coding agent to turn a user's story brief into a complete, loadable payload, including all flavour text. The engine's validator gives errors the agent can act on. The payload contains no executable code.

P9 · Consequences persist; days are chapters

Every state change is an event in an append-only log. Characters remember conversations and what they witnessed. Each in-game day is a chapter: the player's sleep closes it, every character's day is summarised in their own perspective, and those summaries are carried forward (§6.8). Structured state set by hooks always outranks prose memory.

P10 · One rule system

SPECIAL (§5) is the only RPG system. Every mechanical question, from carrying and fighting to persuading, stealing, staying awake and levelling up, is answered with SPECIAL-derived numbers and seeded rolls resolved by the engine.

P11 · Deterministic and replayable

Given the same payload, seed, inputs and recorded LLM responses, a session MUST replay identically.

P12 · Model-agnostic

The engine MUST run against any provider that implements the provider interface, including a scripted fake. Claude via subscription is the default.
